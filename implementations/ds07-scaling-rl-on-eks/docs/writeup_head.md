This appendix describes a working, scaled-down implementation of [Design 07: Scaling RL beyond the small-run regime](#design-scaling-rl). I built it with Terraform in one AWS account and one region: a cluster with one trainer GPU, two rollout GPUs on two nodes, and CPU pools for the controllers and for a sandbox pool. A 0.6-billion-parameter model learns grade-school maths from a verifiable reward, in runs of 20 to 80 steps. Around those runs I built what the design says a platform needs: a step-time breakdown, a rollout queue sized to the trainer, a weight push that is timed by model size, a measurement of the mismatch between the rollout engine and the trainer, an environment tier that scales on its queue, composite alerts and a rollback that changes something, checkpoints that survive the node, a restart policy, and the ladder with its exit criteria.

The order is the one a reader needs: first the context, then the software I used, the topology and instances, and the architecture, then how it is implemented, and then the scenarios I tested, with the commands I ran, what they printed, why the result can be trusted and why you get that output.

**What "scaled down" means here.** The design's run is a 235B mixture-of-experts model, answers up to 64K tokens, thousands of steps and about 200,000 GPU hours. This build has three GPUs, a model 400 times smaller, answers up to 3,072 tokens and runs that cost between 0.05 and 0.5 GPU hours. What carries over is the architecture (a trainer and a rollout fleet joined by NCCL weight transfer, an asynchronous trainer with a staleness bound, a queue-driven environment tier, checkpoints and a restart policy) and the measurements the design asks for at every rung. What does not carry over is any claim about 235B: where a number is extrapolated, the text says so and shows the arithmetic.

**Why standard EKS, and why these tools.** The trainer is TRL's `AsyncGRPOTrainer`, which the library's own documentation describes as decoupling generation from training with a background rollout worker and NCCL weight sync to a vLLM server. That is the design's architecture, so I used it instead of writing a trainer. The rollout fleet is vLLM 0.33 with its NCCL weight-transfer engine. I ran it on standard EKS and not on Auto Mode, because the GPU pools need the NVIDIA node image, a taint, a pinned zone and an instance type chosen per pool.

### Context: the problem, the actors and who talks to whom

**Scenario.** The recipe from Design 06 works on an 8B model with short answers on 32 GPUs. The team now wants a model nearly thirty times larger, answers sixteen times longer, ten times more steps and a budget of about 200,000 GPU hours. The first attempt at one eighth of that scale ran at 11% GPU utilisation, spent 40% of each step waiting for weight sync, and collapsed on day nine with no obvious cause. Nobody could say which of the five things that changed was responsible.

Six terms are used throughout.

- **Rollout.** One answer to one prompt, generated by the rollout fleet. A **group** is the answers to one prompt (eight here), scored together.
- **Rollout fleet.** The vLLM engines that generate answers. They hold a copy of the policy and know nothing about gradients.
- **Weight push.** Sending the trainer's current parameters into the engines over NCCL, once every step or every few steps. Generation is paused while it happens.
- **Staleness.** How many weight versions behind the policy that wrote a sample is, when the trainer uses it. A sample older than the bound is dropped.
- **Rung.** One run in the scaling ladder, which changes exactly one axis (recipe, length, model size, tokens per step, steps) from the rung below.
- **Goodput.** The share of the reserved GPU time that went into steps that counted.

![Who uses the platform and what it runs](diagrams/appendix/a08-1.svg)

**What the picture shows.** Three people use the platform: the research team chooses the recipe and reads the curves, the platform team owns the cluster and the restart policy, and the on-call engineer gets the alert when a run drifts or stalls. Four things run on it: a trainer, a rollout fleet, an environment tier and a state bucket, with a restart policy and a set of measurements around them.

**The path of one training step.**

1. The trainer's rollout worker keeps up to 64 rollouts in flight against the rollout fleet's one address. Each answer comes back with the probability the engine gave every token.
2. When a group is complete it is scored (an exact-match check for maths, a chain of calls into the sandbox pool for code), and its advantages are computed within the group.
3. The scored samples wait in a queue, each stamped with the weight version that produced it.
4. The trainer takes four groups, drops any that are too stale, recomputes the probabilities of the same tokens with its own kernels, and does a forward and backward pass.
5. It then asks the fleet to pause, broadcasts the new weights to every engine over NCCL, and tells the fleet to resume. Every tenth step it also evaluates the policy on 200 held-out problems through the fleet's own API.
6. Every N steps the trainer writes a checkpoint to the node, and a thread copies it to the bucket and marks it complete last.

### Architecture

![The cluster](diagrams/appendix/a08-2.svg)

**What the picture shows.** The trainer pod is alone on its node. It talks to the rollout fleet's leader pod over HTTP (pause, update, resume) and broadcasts weights to both engines over NCCL. The sandbox workers reach the trainer's rollout worker through a Redis list in the system pool. A thread in the trainer pod copies each checkpoint to the state bucket, and a watchdog thread ends the process if it stops making progress.

**What the picture shows.** One step as the trainer's own timers report it. While the trainer does its backward pass the engines generate; while the weights load, the engines generate nothing. On this network the push takes about as long as the whole training computation.

### How it is implemented

**Layer 1: the cluster.** One VPC with three private and three public subnets and one NAT gateway, an EKS 1.37 cluster with the VPC CNI (prefix delegation on, so the CPU nodes can run about a hundred small pods each), and four managed node groups. The GPU groups use the EKS NVIDIA node image and a taint; every pod that wants a GPU tolerates it. A pod-identity association gives the trainer's service account access to one S3 bucket and nothing else.

**Layer 2: the platform.** The device plugin, JobSet, LeaderWorkerSet, KEDA and a Redis. Nothing that belongs to one experiment is installed here: the tests apply the trainer, the rollout fleet and the sandbox pool, so every experiment starts from a known state.

**The rollout fleet.** A LeaderWorkerSet of group size two. The leader pod runs `vllm serve` with two data-parallel engines declared and its own engine; the worker pod runs the same command headless and starts as engine 1. The leader serves the API on port 8000 and a Service in front of it is the trainer's one address. The weight-transfer group spans both engines (world size 3 with the trainer). The fleet gets 70% of each GPU's memory, because the weight receive needs about 2 GiB of buffers outside that budget (S05 shows the failure at 95%).

**The trainer.** A JobSet of one job with one pod. The pod starts from the vLLM image, installs TRL and a matching `transformers`, pins NCCL to the engines' version, and runs the wrapper. The recipe is a set of environment variables (model, learning rate, group size, staleness bound, weight-push interval, queue size, answer length, thinking mode and so on), so a run is described by a handful of lines the test prints before it starts.

**Evaluation and metrics.** Every tenth step the wrapper sends 200 held-out GSM8K problems, four samples each, to the fleet's own API and grades the answers with the same checker as the reward. The compute axis of every curve is GPU hours: the run's own clock times the three GPUs it holds, with the evaluation's time left out. Every logged step is appended to a metrics file with its timestamps.

**Checkpoints and recovery.** Checkpoints go to the node's disk, and a thread uploads each file and then writes a `COMPLETE` marker last, so a half-copied checkpoint is never used. A new trainer pod restores the newest complete checkpoint from the bucket before it starts, and the library resumes the prompt cursor with it. A watchdog thread exits the process if no step finishes for 420 seconds, because NCCL has no timeout and a hang is not restarted by anything.

**The environment tier.** For the code task each answer sends four calls at once: a call is a message on a Redis list, a worker pops it, runs the code in a child process with a time and memory limit, and answers on a result list. KEDA reads the length of the list every five seconds and sets the replica count from it. This is a throwaway pod running a few lines of arithmetic, not a hardened sandbox; Design 01 builds that.

**The ladder.** Rungs are overlay files that extend each other. Each declares one axis and may set only the settings of that axis; a function checks this before anything is scheduled. A gate function takes the overlay's exit criteria and the measured values and returns climb or hold, with every criterion shown.

**Going deeper.**

- **The trainer is an existing library, on purpose.** The design is about the platform around a trainer, not the trainer. What I wrote is everything that makes a run measurable and recoverable: the recipe as data, the evaluation, the metrics, the checkpoint copier, the restore, the rollback and the watchdog.
- **The rollout fleet is one API server for two engines.** vLLM's data-parallel mode puts one OpenAI-compatible server in front of engines on several nodes. The trainer sees one address and one world size, and the weight push reaches both.
- **JSON, not YAML.** The manifests are generated as JSON. A YAML scalar such as `3e-6` is read as a number, and Kubernetes then refuses it as an environment variable's value.

### Where reality differed from the design and from the documentation

These are findings, confirmed on the live system.

- **NCCL versions must match to the patch, and `pip` breaks them.** Installing the trainer's packages on top of the vLLM image downgraded NCCL from 2.31.2 to 2.30.7, the version torch pins. The trainer's group with the engines then failed with "NCCL version mismatch with remote peer", and the HTTP call that starts the group waited forever, because the trainer-side error is raised in a thread and the engines wait for a peer that never joins. The fix is to pin NCCL after the install.
- **The weight receive needs memory outside the KV budget.** At the documented 90% of GPU memory the first push failed with an out-of-memory error: the receive buffers are two copies of 1 GiB. The fleet runs at 70%. S05 reproduces the failure on purpose.
- **The trainer sends fp32 to bf16 engines.** The master weights are fp32 and the manifest declares their dtype, so each push carries twice the bytes the engines keep, and the engines log a warning for every tensor. The push sizes in S05 are bf16, as the design counts them.
- **A health probe that misses once can end the run.** Under load the API server missed its readiness probe, the Service lost its only endpoint, and the trainer got "connection refused" and died. The Service now publishes not-ready addresses, and the probe has a longer timeout and a longer threshold.
- **An over-sized rollout queue wastes the fleet and stalls the sync.** The library's default allows 512 rollouts in flight. With four groups used per step, thousands of samples were generated and dropped as too stale, and the request to pause the fleet waited behind them for up to 130 seconds. S04 measures it. The recipe now allows 64.
- **Four vCPUs are not enough for a vLLM node.** The first rollout nodes (4 vCPUs) starved the API server and the engine; the pool moved to 8 vCPUs. The GPU capacity of a type also differs by zone: `g6.2xlarge` had no capacity in the zone I had chosen for the trainer, and the node group waited 18 minutes with no instance before I looked at its scaling activity.
- **The first evaluation measured the previous run's policy.** The trainer's own start-of-training hook pushes the first weights, and my evaluation hook ran before it. The starting score of a new run was the last run's final score. The evaluation now runs at the start of the first step.
- **Math-verify cannot run in threads.** Its timeouts use signals. The evaluation sends its requests from threads and grades the answers afterwards in the main thread.
- **The staleness metric counts weight versions, not steps.** Pushing every fourth step lowers the count by four and the samples are not fresher; S03 shows both units.
- **Two flag combinations that look right are refused.** On the data-parallel leader, naming `--data-parallel-start-rank 0` turns on a mode in which the headless worker is rejected, and a Service named like the LeaderWorkerSet replaces the headless Service the group's pods are found by.
- **A tool job can hold the GPU forever.** A benchmark whose peer died blocked in NCCL for two and a half hours and kept every later job pending. Every tool job now has a deadline and is deleted when it is exceeded.

### 5. The scenarios I tested

**Scenario.** The design makes specific promises. I turned each into a scenario that runs real commands against the live cluster, trains, breaks and measures, and decides pass or fail from the output, with no human judgement in the loop.

**How to read this section.**

- Lines starting with `kubectl` and `aws` run against the cluster and the account. Lines starting with `python` are the analysis scripts of the test, run over the records the runs wrote; their output is computed from those records. A line that names a JobSet is a training run whose settings are listed under it.
- A run of "r1-center" is the baseline recipe: Qwen3-0.6B, GSM8K, learning rate 3e-6, 80 steps, eight answers per group, four groups per step, at most 64 rollouts in flight. Every other run changes the setting its title names and nothing else.
- Training is noisy. A held-out score is the mean of 800 samples and has a standard error of about 0.02, so differences below that are not claimed.
- Some scenarios read back a run an earlier scenario made. The transcript says so.
