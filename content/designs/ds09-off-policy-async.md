This design answers one question: *how do we train on data generated by older versions of the model, by different models, or by filtered selections of the model's outputs, and know when that data helps, when it hurts, and how to keep the resulting instabilities under control?* Asynchronous RL ([Design 07](#design-scaling-rl)) makes almost every sample a little off-policy; replay buffers, teacher data and filtering ([Design 06](#design-post-training)) make some samples very off-policy. This design builds the intuition, the metrics and the controls for all of them.

The short version: *always know which policy produced each token, and measure how far it is from the policy being trained*. Every token carries its **behavior policy** (who generated it, which version, its log-probability). The trainer separates three policies: the **behavior** policy that generated the data, a **proximal** policy that anchors the update, and the **target** policy being trained. Corrections are applied in two separate places (engine mismatch and staleness), and a small set of off-policy health metrics (importance-ratio quantiles, effective sample size, clipped and masked fractions, version age) drive automatic controls: lower the staleness limit, drop a batch, cut the learning rate, or roll back.

### The use case and requirements

**Scenario.** The RL team moved to fully asynchronous training to keep GPUs busy, and it's 2.4× faster. But the results are inconsistent: one run matches the synchronous baseline, another with "the same settings" collapses on day two, and a third quietly ends four points lower. At the same time, researchers want to reuse last month's expensive agent trajectories, mix in teacher solutions for hard problems, and keep dynamic sampling. Nobody can say how off-policy any of this data is, or which of it is safe.

**What the platform must do.**

- **Record provenance for every token:** which model and version produced it, from which engine, with what log-probability and sampling settings.
- **Measure off-policyness per batch:** how far the data is from the current policy, in numbers that predict trouble.
- **Correct consistently:** one implementation of the corrections, applied the same way in every trainer, with the policies kept separate.
- **Control instability automatically:** respond to warning signs during a run, not in a post-mortem.
- **Build intuition with experiments:** make it cheap to sweep staleness, data age and data mixtures, and see where each recipe breaks.
- **Support reuse:** replay buffers and other-policy data with explicit rules for what can be mixed in and how much.

**Out of scope:** the base objective and rewards (Design 06), the rollout systems themselves (Design 07).

### Where off-policy data comes from

**Scenario.** In a design review, "off-policy" means six different things to six people. The team needs a shared vocabulary and a picture of how far from the current policy each source usually is.

![Where off-policy data comes from](diagrams/designs/ds09-1.svg)

**What the picture shows.** Sources of off-policy data, roughly ordered from mildly to strongly off-policy.

- **Engine mismatch:** same weights, different engines (vLLM or SGLang vs FSDP or Megatron). Technically off-policy even in synchronous training; small but always present.
- **Staleness from asynchrony:** samples generated by a policy that's *k* updates behind the trainer. Grows with *k* and with the learning rate.
- **Partial rollouts:** one answer generated across several policy versions (paused, then resumed under newer weights).
- **Selection and filtering:** dynamic sampling, rejection sampling and best-of-n change *which* samples are trained on. Each token is still the policy's own, but the batch no longer follows the policy's distribution.
- **Replay of older data:** trajectories from earlier in the run or from previous runs, possibly hundreds of updates old.
- **Other policies:** teacher models, other checkpoints, human demonstrations. Their tokens may be very unlikely under the current policy.

**Going deeper.**

- **Off-policyness is a distance, not a yes/no:** it's measured per token as the log-ratio between the current policy and whatever produced the token, and it differs a lot between tokens in the same answer.
- **The same staleness can be harmless or fatal:** five updates at a tiny learning rate on a stable run move the policy less than one update with a large learning rate during a fast phase. Measure the distance, not just the version gap.

### Three policies: behavior, proximal and target

**Scenario.** The trainer computes PPO's ratio against "the old policy," which in the synchronous code meant "the policy that generated the data." In async mode that's the rollout engine's log-probability from four versions ago, so the clipping range now does two jobs at once (correcting staleness and limiting the update) and does neither well.

![Three policies: behavior, proximal and target](diagrams/designs/ds09-2.svg)

**What the picture shows.** Separating the policies so each correction does one job.

- **Behavior policy (μ):** the policy that actually sampled the tokens, as seen by the inference engine, at some older version. Its log-probabilities are recorded at sampling time.
- **Proximal policy (π_prox):** a recent version of the policy, computed by the *training* engine, used as the anchor for clipping. It defines the trust region: how far one update may move.
- **Target policy (π_θ):** the parameters being updated now.
- **Two ratios, two jobs:** π_θ / π_prox is clipped (PPO's trust region); π_prox / μ is an importance weight that corrects for staleness and engine mismatch, truncated or masked when it's extreme.
- **Decoupled objectives:** this separation is the core of decoupled PPO, used by asynchronous systems such as AReaL. One reported ablation on a 1.5B reasoning model found naive PPO losing about 19 AIME24 points at a staleness of 4, while the decoupled objective stayed close to the synchronous result.
- **The missing-old-logits problem:** π_prox needs the training engine's log-probabilities for an older version. If those were never computed (weights already overwritten, partial rollouts spanning versions), staleness and engine-mismatch corrections get tangled together. Recent work names this failure and proposes repairs; the platform avoids it by computing π_prox log-probabilities on arrival.

**The code.** A decoupled, token-level loss with a truncated behavior weight (PyTorch).

```python
import torch

def decoupled_ppo_loss(logp_target, logp_prox, logp_behavior, advantages, mask,
                       clip_low=0.2, clip_high=0.28, is_cap=3.0, mask_above=10.0):
    """
    logp_target:   log π_θ(token)      (requires grad)
    logp_prox:     log π_prox(token)   training engine, recent version (no grad)
    logp_behavior: log μ(token)        recorded by the rollout engine at sampling time
    """
    ratio = torch.exp(logp_target - logp_prox)                       # trust region #!
    behavior_w = torch.exp(logp_prox - logp_behavior).detach()       # staleness + engine mismatch #!
    keep = mask * (behavior_w < mask_above).float()                  # mask extreme tokens (MIS)
    behavior_w = behavior_w.clamp(max=is_cap)                        # truncate the rest (TIS)

    surr1 = ratio * advantages
    surr2 = ratio.clamp(1 - clip_low, 1 + clip_high) * advantages
    per_token = -behavior_w * torch.min(surr1, surr2)
    loss = (per_token * keep).sum() / keep.sum().clamp(min=1)        # token-level mean

    stats = {"offpolicy/masked_frac": 1 - keep.sum().item() / mask.sum().item(),
             "offpolicy/clipped_frac": (((ratio - 1).abs() > clip_low) * mask).sum().item() / mask.sum().item()}
    return loss, stats
```

**Going deeper.**

- **Choose π_prox by convenience and cost:** the version that generated the data (needs one extra forward pass per sample on arrival) or the trainer's state at the start of the step (free, but further from the data). Record which one a run uses.
- **Sequence-level ratios** (GSPO-style) are steadier for long answers and MoE models, because one extreme token can't dominate; they also hide which tokens are off. Keep token-level metrics even when the loss is sequence-level.

### When off-policy data helps and when it hurts

**Scenario.** A researcher asks a simple question: "Can I set staleness to 8?" The honest answer depends on the learning rate, the correction, the task and how fast the policy is changing, and the platform should be able to show it.

![When off-policy data helps and when it hurts](diagrams/designs/ds09-3.svg)

**What the picture shows.** The trade-off between throughput and the quality of each sample, and where recipes typically land.

- **It helps:** throughput (no GPU waits for the slowest rollout), more samples per GPU hour, reuse of expensive trajectories, and access to behavior the current policy can't yet produce (teacher solutions to hard problems).
- **It hurts through bias:** uncorrected, the update optimizes the wrong policy's objective; the further the data, the larger the error.
- **It hurts through variance:** corrected, a few tokens with huge importance weights dominate the gradient; effective sample size falls, and the update becomes noisy even when unbiased.
- **The safe region depends on how fast the policy moves:** small learning rates and slow phases tolerate more staleness. Early training and sudden capability jumps tolerate less.
- **Good corrections move the cliff:** recent work describes a "prosperity before collapse" pattern, where stale data stays as useful as fresh data up to a point and then fails abruptly. A method that suppresses only the extreme importance weights (M2PO) reported stable training with data at least 256 updates old.

**Data sources at a glance.**

| Data source | Usually helps when | Usually hurts when | Control |
|---|---|---|---|
| Async staleness 1–4 | Long tails waste GPU time | LR high, fast-changing phase | Decoupled loss, bounded staleness |
| Partial rollouts | Very long answers | Versions not recorded per span | Per-span versions, behavior weights |
| Filtering / dynamic sampling | Many zero-signal groups | Filter correlates with a behavior you don't want | Read capability from held-out evals |
| Replay buffer | Rollouts are expensive (agents) | Old data dominates; low ESS | Age limits, mixing ratio, ESS floor |
| Teacher / other policy | Policy can't solve hard prompts yet | Tokens very unlikely under the policy | Small ratio, SFT-style loss, anneal out |

**Going deeper.**

- **Every recipe has a staleness curve:** final held-out score versus allowed staleness, for each correction. That curve, measured at small scale, is the evidence for the staleness setting at large scale.
- **Distance, not age, predicts failure:** across runs, the behavior KL and the importance-ratio tail predict collapse better than the version gap. Configure limits in both.

### Bounded staleness in an asynchronous pipeline

**Scenario.** Rollout engines get new weights every few minutes, but one slow engine still serves version 112 while the trainer is at 131. Its samples are accepted because nothing checks.

![Bounded staleness in an asynchronous pipeline](diagrams/designs/ds09-4.svg)

**What the picture shows.** How versions flow through the pipeline and where staleness is enforced.

- **Version tags:** every weight push has a version; every token records the version that produced it (and partial rollouts record a list of spans).
- **Admission at the buffer:** samples older than the limit are rejected or sent to the replay pool, not into the main batch.
- **Admission at the rollout scheduler:** engines more than *k* versions behind stop receiving new prompts until they reload, so stale data isn't generated in the first place.
- **Batch composition:** the trainer can cap the share of a batch at each age (for example, at most 30% at the maximum allowed age).
- **Adaptive limits:** the limit can tighten automatically when the off-policy metrics degrade (see the controller below).

**The YAML.** The asynchronous pipeline's policy, read by the rollout scheduler, the buffer and the trainer.

```yaml
# recipes/assistant-v13/async-policy.yaml
async:
  max_staleness: 4                      # versions; hard limit at admission #!
  max_behavior_kl: 0.02                 # distance limit, checked per batch
  engine_stops_sampling_when_behind: 4  # don't generate what we'd reject
  batch_age_mix: { "0-1": ">=0.5", "4": "<=0.3" }
  partial_rollouts: { enabled: true, record_version_spans: true }
correction:
  objective: decoupled_ppo
  proximal_policy: version_of_sample    # π_prox computed by trainer on arrival #!
  behavior_weight: { truncate_at: 3.0, mask_above: 10.0, level: token }
controller:
  enabled: true
  rules: recipes/assistant-v13/offpolicy-controller.yaml
```

**Going deeper.**

- **Compute π_prox on arrival:** the trainer (or a dedicated log-prob worker on training-engine kernels) scores each sample with the weights of the version that generated it before those weights are discarded. This avoids the missing-old-logits problem and costs one forward pass per sample.
- **Keep a few old weight versions in memory or on fast storage** for exactly this purpose, sized to the staleness limit.

### Filtering is off-policy too

**Scenario.** Dynamic sampling keeps only prompts with mixed outcomes, and rejection sampling keeps only correct answers for an SFT step. Both "use the model's own outputs," so the team assumed they were on-policy. After a month, the model avoids a whole class of problems it used to attempt.

![Filtering is off-policy too](diagrams/designs/ds09-5.svg)

**What the picture shows.** How filters change the training distribution, and what to do about it.

- **Prompt-level filters (dynamic sampling, pass-rate curation):** change which tasks are trained on. Usually benign for learning signal, but they shift what "average reward" means and can drop whole task types as they become easy or hard.
- **Sample-level filters (rejection sampling, best-of-n):** train only on selected answers. Equivalent to a different behavior policy (the "policy plus filter"), biased toward whatever the filter favors, including length, style or verifier quirks.
- **Token-level filters (masking extreme ratios, high-entropy tokens only):** change which tokens carry gradient. Useful for stability, but a large masked fraction means much of the data isn't being learned from.
- **Controls:** report filter rates per task type over time, cap how much any filter can remove, evaluate on unfiltered held-out sets, and treat a large rise in any filter rate as a signal, not just a mechanism.

**The code.** Logging what filters remove, by task type, so shifts are visible (Python).

```python
from collections import Counter

def filter_report(groups, step: int, log):
    """groups: list of dicts with task_type and per-sample rewards; dynamic sampling drops all-equal groups."""
    seen, dropped = Counter(), Counter()
    kept = []
    for g in groups:
        seen[g["task_type"]] += 1
        if len(set(g["rewards"])) == 1:                 # zero-variance group: no signal
            dropped[g["task_type"]] += 1
            continue
        kept.append(g)
    for t in seen:
        log(f"filter/drop_rate/{t}", dropped[t] / seen[t], step)   # watch these drift #!
    log("filter/kept_fraction", len(kept) / max(len(groups), 1), step)
    return kept
```

**Going deeper.**

- **Best-of-n then SFT is a strong off-policy push:** it moves the model toward its own best samples quickly, and toward the verifier's blind spots just as quickly. Keep the selected fraction and the verifier under review.
- **Masked fraction is a cost:** if 5% of tokens are masked for extreme ratios, 5% of the rollout compute produced nothing. Track it alongside throughput.

### Replay buffers and data from other policies

**Scenario.** Agent trajectories cost $3 each to generate. The team wants to reuse successful ones from last month and add teacher-written solutions for the hardest 5% of tasks. The first attempt mixes them in at 40% and the policy's entropy collapses within 300 steps.

![Replay buffers and data from other policies](diagrams/designs/ds09-6.svg)

**What the picture shows.** Rules for mixing in data the current policy didn't generate.

- **Separate pools:** fresh on-policy data, replay (own older data), and external (teacher, other checkpoints, human). Each has its own mixing ratio in the batch.
- **Age and distance limits:** replay samples are eligible only within an age limit and when their behavior KL to the current policy is below a threshold; the rest are retired.
- **Prioritize informative samples:** successes on hard tasks and samples with large advantages, re-scored with current values where possible.
- **External data with its own loss:** teacher tokens can be very unlikely under the policy, so importance weights explode. Use a separate, small-weight SFT-style term (or on-policy distillation, Design 06) rather than pushing them through the RL loss, and reduce the weight as the policy learns to solve those tasks itself.
- **ESS floor:** if a batch's effective sample size falls below a set fraction of its size, the mixing ratio of off-policy pools drops automatically.

**The code.** A replay buffer with age and distance limits, sampled at a fixed ratio (Python).

```python
import random
from dataclasses import dataclass

@dataclass
class Sample:
    tokens: list[int]
    behavior_version: int
    behavior_kl: float          # refreshed periodically vs the current policy
    advantage: float
    source: str                 # "fresh" | "replay" | "teacher"

class MixedBuffer:
    def __init__(self, max_age=64, max_kl=0.05, mix={"fresh": 0.8, "replay": 0.15, "teacher": 0.05}):
        self.pools = {k: [] for k in mix}
        self.max_age, self.max_kl, self.mix = max_age, max_kl, mix

    def add(self, s: Sample):
        self.pools[s.source].append(s)

    def sample(self, n: int, current_version: int) -> list[Sample]:
        self.pools["replay"] = [s for s in self.pools["replay"]                 # retire old/far data #!
                                if current_version - s.behavior_version <= self.max_age
                                and s.behavior_kl <= self.max_kl]
        batch = []
        for src, frac in self.mix.items():
            pool = sorted(self.pools[src], key=lambda s: -abs(s.advantage))    # informative first
            batch += pool[: int(n * frac)]
        random.shuffle(batch)
        return batch

    def lower_offpolicy_share(self, factor=0.5):                               # called by the controller
        moved = sum(v for k, v in self.mix.items() if k != "fresh") * (1 - factor)
        for k in self.mix:
            self.mix[k] = self.mix[k] * factor if k != "fresh" else self.mix[k] + moved
```

**Going deeper.**

- **Off-policy data can rescue exploration:** when the policy solves 0% of a task family, a few teacher solutions provide the first positive signal. Anneal them out once the policy succeeds on its own.
- **Re-score replay rewards with the current verifier:** a verifier fix should apply to old data too, or the buffer keeps teaching the old exploit.

### Diagnostics: measuring off-policyness

**Scenario.** Two runs look identical on reward. One has 0.3% of tokens with importance ratio above 3; the other has 4%, rising. Only one of them will survive the week.

![Diagnostics: measuring off-policyness](diagrams/designs/ds09-7.svg)

**What the picture shows.** The off-policy metrics every run reports per step, and what each predicts.

- **Importance-ratio quantiles (p50, p99, max) and tail fraction:** the share of tokens with ratio above 2 or 3. A growing tail is the earliest warning of collapse.
- **Effective sample size (ESS):** how many samples the weighted batch is "worth." ESS / N near 1 means the weights are even; a falling ESS means a few samples dominate.
- **Behavior KL:** the estimated KL between the behavior and current policies, per source (fresh, replay, teacher) and per age bucket.
- **Clipped fraction and masked fraction:** how much of the batch is being cut by the trust region or dropped by the behavior weight.
- **Version age histogram:** the distribution of staleness actually trained on, versus the configured limit.
- **Engine mismatch at zero staleness:** the same metrics for samples with age 0, to separate engine mismatch from staleness.

**The code.** Effective sample size and ratio-tail metrics from the stored log-probabilities (PyTorch).

```python
import torch

def offpolicy_metrics(logp_prox, logp_behavior, mask, ages):
    lr = (logp_prox - logp_behavior)[mask.bool()]
    w = lr.exp()
    ess = (w.sum() ** 2) / (w.pow(2).sum() * w.numel())            # ESS / N, in (0, 1] #!
    m = {
        "offpolicy/ess_frac": ess.item(),
        "offpolicy/ratio_p99": w.quantile(0.99).item(),
        "offpolicy/ratio_gt3_frac": (w > 3).float().mean().item(),
        "offpolicy/behavior_kl": ((w - 1) - lr).mean().item(),     # k3 estimator
    }
    for a in sorted(set(ages.tolist())):                            # per-age KL: is staleness the cause?
        sel = (ages == a).unsqueeze(-1).expand_as(mask).bool() & mask.bool()
        if sel.any():
            l = (logp_prox - logp_behavior)[sel]
            m[f"offpolicy/behavior_kl_age{a}"] = ((l.exp() - 1) - l).mean().item()
    return m
```

**Going deeper.**

- **Per-token ESS can mislead for long sequences;** also compute it at the sequence level (product or mean of log-ratios per answer), which is what matters for sequence-level objectives.
- **Plot metrics against age:** if the behavior KL grows steeply with age, the staleness limit is too loose for the current learning rate; if it's high even at age 0, the problem is engine mismatch, not staleness.

### Controlling instabilities automatically

**Scenario.** At 3 a.m., the ratio tail fraction crosses 2% and keeps climbing. By the time someone looks at 9 a.m., the run has collapsed and the last healthy checkpoint is six hours old.

![Controlling instabilities automatically](diagrams/designs/ds09-8.svg)

**What the picture shows.** A controller that reads the off-policy metrics and applies graduated responses.

- **Level 1, tighten:** lower the staleness limit, lower the share of replay and external data, or make engines reload sooner.
- **Level 2, protect the update:** skip a batch whose ESS or ratio tail is far outside the normal range; lower the learning rate temporarily.
- **Level 3, recover:** roll back to the last checkpoint whose metrics were healthy, apply the tighter settings, and continue; alert a person.
- **Hysteresis:** loosen settings again only after metrics have been healthy for a while, so the controller doesn't oscillate.
- **Everything logged:** every controller action is recorded as an event on the run's timeline, with the metric values that triggered it.

**The YAML.** The controller's rules, versioned with the recipe.

```yaml
# recipes/assistant-v13/offpolicy-controller.yaml
window_steps: 10
rules:
- name: ratio-tail-rising
  when: { metric: offpolicy/ratio_gt3_frac, above: 0.01, for_steps: 5 }
  then:
  - { set: async.max_staleness, to: "max(1, current - 2)" }       # tighten first #!
  - { set: buffer.mix.replay, multiply: 0.5 }
- name: low-ess
  when: { metric: offpolicy/ess_frac, below: 0.6 }
  then:
  - { action: skip_batch }
  - { set: optim.lr, multiply: 0.5, restore_after_steps: 50 }
- name: collapse-warning
  when:
    all:
    - { metric: offpolicy/ratio_gt3_frac, above: 0.03 }
    - { metric: health/entropy_slope, below: -0.002 }
  then:
  - { action: rollback, to: last_healthy_checkpoint }             #!
  - { action: page, team: rl-oncall }
relax:
  healthy_for_steps: 200
  step_back: { async.max_staleness: +1, buffer.mix.replay: "*1.25" }
```

**The code.** The controller loop that applies those rules (Python sketch).

```python
def control_step(step, metrics, rules, state, actions):
    for rule in rules:
        if rule.triggered(metrics, state.history):
            for a in rule.then:
                actions.apply(a)                                  # update recipe-live config
                state.events.append({"step": step, "rule": rule.name, "action": a, "metrics": metrics})
            state.last_trigger = step
            return                                                # one rule per step
    if step - state.last_trigger > state.relax_after:
        actions.relax_one_notch()
```

**Going deeper.**

- **Healthy checkpoints are tagged automatically:** a checkpoint is "healthy" if the off-policy and health metrics in the surrounding window were within limits; rollback targets only those.
- **Test the controller on purpose:** inject staleness or a learning-rate spike in a small run and confirm the controller catches it before the reward moves.

### Building intuition: off-policy experiments

**Scenario.** New team members learn the effects of staleness by breaking production runs. The team wants a cheap, repeatable way to see the effects instead.

![Building intuition: off-policy experiments](diagrams/designs/ds09-9.svg)

**What the picture shows.** A small, standard experiment harness that turns off-policy questions into curves.

- **Staleness injection:** a synchronous run with an artificial delay: train on samples from *k* versions ago. Isolates staleness from every other async effect.
- **Sweeps:** staleness × correction method × learning rate, on a small model and a fixed task set; each cell is a short run.
- **Outputs:** the staleness curve (final held-out score vs *k*) for each correction, the steps-to-collapse, and the off-policy metrics at the moment each run broke.
- **Data-source sweeps:** the replay ratio and the teacher ratio, with the same outputs.
- **A reference result library:** every sweep's curves are stored with the recipe and model size, so "can I set staleness to 8?" is answered by a lookup.

**The YAML.** A staleness sweep as an Indexed Job: each index is one cell of the grid, admitted by Kueue at low priority.

```yaml
apiVersion: batch/v1
kind: Job
metadata:
  name: staleness-sweep-2026-11
  namespace: rl-research
  labels:
    kueue.x-k8s.io/queue-name: research-small
    kueue.x-k8s.io/priority-class: sweep-low
spec:
  completions: 24                      # 6 staleness × 2 corrections × 2 learning rates
  parallelism: 8
  completionMode: Indexed
  backoffLimit: 0
  template:
    spec:
      restartPolicy: Never
      containers:
      - name: cell
        image: registry.example.com/rl/trainer:2026.11
        command: ["python3", "-m", "rl.sweeps.staleness"]
        args:
        - --index=$(JOB_COMPLETION_INDEX)
        - --grid=staleness:0,1,2,4,8,16;correction:naive,decoupled;lr:1e-6,3e-6
        - --model=registry://small-1.5b@v3
        - --inject=delayed-sync          # isolate staleness from other async effects #!
        - --steps=400
        resources: { limits: { nvidia.com/gpu: 8 } }
```

**Going deeper.**

- **Re-run the sweep when the recipe changes meaningfully:** a new objective, a new model family or a much larger learning rate can move the cliff.
- **Check at one larger scale:** confirm two or three cells of the sweep on the target model before trusting the small-scale curve.

### The platform on Kubernetes

**Scenario.** The trainer, rollout engines, buffer, log-prob workers and controller were added piece by piece; nobody owns the versions flowing between them.

![The platform on Kubernetes](diagrams/designs/ds09-10.svg)

**What the picture shows.** The components and the data each passes on.

- **Rollout engines (LeaderWorkerSet):** sample with version tags and record behavior log-probabilities; refuse new prompts when too far behind.
- **Weight distribution:** pushes versioned weights to engines and keeps the last *k* versions available for log-prob workers ([Design 07](#design-scaling-rl)).
- **Log-prob workers:** compute π_prox log-probabilities on arrival with the training engine's kernels and the sample's version.
- **Sample buffer:** fresh, replay and external pools; admission rules for age and distance; mixing ratios; persisted with checkpoints.
- **Trainer (JobSet):** decoupled loss, off-policy metrics, checkpoints tagged healthy or not.
- **Controller:** reads metrics, changes live settings, triggers rollbacks, writes events.
- **Experiment harness:** staleness and mixing sweeps on the research queue.

**Going deeper.**

- **Live settings are versioned too:** the controller's changes are written to a per-run settings record, so a run's effective configuration at any step can be reconstructed exactly.
- **One correction library:** the loss, weights and metrics live in one shared package used by every trainer (TRL for small runs, verl or Megatron-based trainers for large ones), so "decoupled with truncation 3.0" means the same thing everywhere.

### Rollout plan, trade-offs and open questions

**Rollout.**

- **Phase 1, provenance and metrics:** per-token behavior version and log-probabilities, off-policy metrics on every run, version-age histograms.
- **Phase 2, consistent corrections:** the shared correction library with decoupled loss; π_prox computed on arrival; bounded staleness at both the buffer and the scheduler.
- **Phase 3, control:** the controller with graduated responses, healthy-checkpoint tagging and automatic rollback.
- **Phase 4, reuse and intuition:** replay and external pools with limits; the sweep harness and reference result library.

**Trade-offs we accepted.**

- **Computing π_prox on arrival** costs an extra forward pass per sample, in exchange for clean separation of staleness and engine mismatch.
- **Conservative default staleness** (1–4) gives up some throughput for predictable training; larger limits are used only with evidence from the sweep library.
- **Automatic rollbacks** sometimes discard good progress after a false alarm; we accept that over losing a whole run.
- **External data through a separate loss** is less elegant than one unified objective, but much easier to keep stable.

**Open questions.**

- **Distance-adaptive staleness:** should the limit be expressed only in behavior KL, letting the version gap float freely?
- **Better estimators for long sequences:** token-level weights are noisy, sequence-level weights explode; which middle ground (segments, turns) works best for long-horizon agents ([Design 08](#design-long-horizon))?
- **Very old data:** with outlier-only suppression reported to work at hundreds of updates of staleness, how much of last month's data can we safely reuse?
- **Filters as policies:** can we model the "policy plus filter" explicitly and correct for selection bias, rather than just monitoring it?
